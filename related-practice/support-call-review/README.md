# An unexpected support call: three fictional decisions

Practice deciding what to do when someone asks to see or control an agency computer. Allow about 10 minutes. The cases are separate and invented. They do not describe an agency incident or a test of Teams or any other software.

Read each case and answer the questions before opening the [authored answer guide](AUTHORED-KEY.md). For each answer, separate what the case says from what you would still need to check.

## Case 1: the request

A person calls an agency employee on Teams. The display name looks ordinary. The caller says they are from the agency's IT provider and need the employee to share their screen before a renewal meeting. The employee was not expecting support. The caller offers a number to call back, but the employee cannot reach the provider using the number the agency already had on file.

1. What can the employee say about the caller's identity and the request so far?
2. What should happen before sharing the screen? What if the known contact route does not answer?
3. Who should handle the next step, and what should stay unknown for now?

## Case 2: a confirmed request with a limit

In a separate case, an employee ends an unexpected call and calls the agency's IT provider using a number the agency had saved before the request. The provider confirms an appointment and the caller's identity. On that independently initiated call, the provider asks to view a blank test window. The agency's support procedure permits that specific step after verification. The provider then asks to see the whole desktop and to control the computer. The employee has not agreed to either request.

1. What has been confirmed, and what does that confirmation cover?
2. Does permission to view the blank window automatically authorize the whole desktop or remote control?
3. What should the employee do before the request expands?

## Case 3: screen sharing already happened

In a third, separate case, an employee accepted an unexpected support call and shared their screen for about 30 seconds. The sharing session has ended. The employee did not knowingly grant remote control, but does not remember what was visible or know who the caller was.

1. What is known? What is still unknown about the caller, visible information and remote control?
2. Who should be told, and what facts can the employee report through the agency's approved channel?
3. Does the screen share alone prove that a breach occurred or that no remote control occurred?

## Keep the practice fictional

Do not call a number, share a screen, change an account or contact a real provider as part of this exercise. Do not put passwords, one-time codes, client records or actual support numbers into your answers or this public repository. Keep any completed notes in an agency-approved location.

For a real suspicious request, use your agency's existing incident procedure and contact the responsible IT or security provider through an independently known route. The provider can help determine what happened and what response is appropriate.

## Background and limits

[Microsoft's 5 November 2026 threat report](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2026/11/05/email-threat-landscape-q3-2026-trends-and-insights/) describes attacks using Teams calls, screen sharing and remote-support tools. Microsoft does not identify an insurance-agency victim in that discussion, and its call-attempt counts do not establish successful attacker interactions. These cases are our invented teaching examples, not Microsoft scenarios or an endorsement.
