# Authored review key

Try the [cases](source-cases.md) first. These are discussion answers, not evidence that a real agency has these procedures or that a technical control works.

## Q1: an unexpected document

The message claims a document is waiting, but the producer did not expect it. The sender's identity, the document and the code's destination are not confirmed by the note. A familiar name and the message's phone number do not independently confirm the request.

Do not scan the code just to find out. Ask the carrier through a number or portal already known to the agency, independently of the message. If the request cannot be confirmed, use the agency's suspicious-message reporting process. The note alone does not establish that the request is fraudulent.

## Q2: a setup you started

The staff member initiated an agency-approved setup through the approved portal and recognizes the instructions. That context differs from Q1. Follow the agency's approved setup process; if a screen or request is unexpected, pause and ask the administrator through a trusted route. A QR code by itself is not proof of a safe or fraudulent request.

## Q3: a scan already happened

The coworker reports scanning an unexpected code with a personal phone. Whether a page opened, credentials were entered, a sign-in was approved, a download occurred or a payment was submitted remains unknown. A scan, a page opening and providing credentials are different actions. Do not assume either compromise or that nothing happened.

Promptly contact the agency's IT provider or security lead through a trusted route and follow the incident procedure. Report the device, approximate time, the scan and each known or uncertain action. Preserve the message as instructed by that procedure. Do not send passwords, one-time codes, customer records or the suspicious link to an unverified contact. If money was sent, contact the payment provider through a verified number promptly and follow the agency's incident process.
