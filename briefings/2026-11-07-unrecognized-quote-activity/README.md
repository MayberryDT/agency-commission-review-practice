# Unrecognized quote activity in a carrier portal

A carrier portal shows quote requests nobody on your team recognizes. That does not prove someone broke in. It is still worth reporting promptly to the person who handles security incidents for your agency and, when appropriate, to the carrier through a contact your agency already trusts. If you can't reach the agency contact, use the backup in your response process. If there is no backup, contact the carrier through that established route.

In March 2021, an independent agent told State Auto that roughly 20,000 quote requests had appeared that the agent had not submitted. The carrier investigated and found unauthorized access beginning earlier that month. New York's Department of Financial Services describes the case in its [2025 consent order](https://www.dfs.ny.gov/system/files/documents/2025/10/ea20251014-co-state-auto-mutual-ins-company.pdf) (page 4, paragraphs 12 to 14). This is a historical example, not a claim about a current State Auto problem. You do not need to wait for a number like that before reporting something unusual.

## What to pass along

Use your agency's approved incident-reporting process. Give the responsible person what you actually know:

- Which carrier portal and agency account or role showed the activity.
- What looked unusual, including an approximate count if one is available. Say if the count is only a sample.
- When you first noticed it, the time zone and the last known normal activity, if known.
- Where you saw it and whether a colleague or an approved process has confirmed any of the requests.
- What you have already done and whom you have told.

Keep unknown details marked as unknown. Preserve permitted observations or logs according to your agency's process. Don't delete activity records or put passwords, authentication codes or customer information into this public repository. Save any screenshots or sensitive records only in an agency-approved location and share them through an approved channel.

NIST's [small-business cybersecurity guidance](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/CSWP/NIST.CSWP.50.pdf) recommends documenting an incident, including when it was first detected, relevant evidence and actions taken, and contacting the experts named in the incident response plan (page 19). The guide is written for businesses without employees and also says it can be useful to very small businesses and consultants. The steps here are Cirlet's application to an agency scenario, not a regulator-approved procedure.

## Let the right people investigate

An approved batch or another staff member's work could explain some activity. Ask the responsible person to check, but don't wait for a complete explanation before making the initial report. The carrier may control the portal and its logs. Ask through an established carrier contact what it can check and what the agency should do next; don't assume every portal provides the same history or controls.

Follow your agency's and carrier's instructions on pausing access, changing credentials and preserving evidence. The people responsible for the response should decide whether containment, outside help or notifications are needed. A normal-looking login or a single explanation is not a complete security review.

## Make the next report easier

Before there is a problem, record the agency's security contact and backup, the carrier contact route, where an incident report belongs and who can approve an access change. Check that staff can find those details without relying on a suspicious message.

For a different portal risk, try the [fictional search-result review](../../related-practice/search-result-review/README.md). It covers an unfamiliar login address, not unexplained activity inside a portal.
